A — Crypto Terms

Address

Wallets & Security Beginner

Simple Definition

A crypto address is a string of letters and numbers that acts like an account number for sending and receiving digital assets on a blockchain.

How It Works

Addresses are generated from a wallet's public key through cryptographic hashing. Anyone can see an address and the transactions linked to it on a block explorer, but only the holder of the matching private key can authorize spending from it. Formats vary by network — Bitcoin addresses typically start with 1, 3, or bc1, while Ethereum addresses start with 0x.

Example

To receive Bitcoin from a friend, you share your wallet's receiving address rather than any personal information, and the funds appear once the transaction confirms.

Why It Matters

Understanding addresses helps users avoid sending funds to the wrong network or mistyping a character, either of which can make coins permanently unrecoverable.

Risks / Limitations

Addresses are pseudonymous, not anonymous — transaction history tied to an address is public and permanent.

Airdrop

Tokenomics Beginner

Simple Definition

An airdrop is a distribution of free tokens sent directly to wallet addresses, often used to reward early users or promote a new project.

How It Works

Projects set eligibility criteria — such as holding a certain token, using a specific app, or being an early tester — then send tokens to qualifying addresses at no cost to the recipient. Some airdrops require completing simple actions like connecting a wallet or signing a message to prove eligibility.

Example

A new decentralized exchange might airdrop its governance token to everyone who traded on the platform before its official token launch.

Why It Matters

Airdrops can reward loyal community members and help new protocols distribute ownership more broadly than a traditional sale.

Risks / Limitations

Fraudulent airdrops are a common phishing tactic; interacting with unsolicited token offers or connecting a wallet to unknown sites can lead to stolen funds.

Related Terms

  • Token Burn
  • Contract Address

Algorithm

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

In crypto, an algorithm is a defined set of rules a computer follows to complete a task, such as securing a network, mining a block, or executing a trade.

How It Works

Blockchains rely on multiple algorithms working together, including consensus algorithms that validate transactions and cryptographic algorithms that keep data secure. Trading platforms also use algorithms to automate strategies based on price data.

Example

Bitcoin's mining algorithm, SHA-256, requires computers to repeatedly guess a number until they find one that produces a valid block hash.

Why It Matters

The choice of algorithm affects a network's security, speed, and energy use, making it a core design decision for any blockchain.

Algorithmic Stablecoin

Stablecoins Intermediate

Simple Definition

An algorithmic stablecoin is a token designed to hold a steady value, typically pegged to a currency like the US dollar, using code-driven supply adjustments instead of holding cash or other assets as backing.

How It Works

Rather than being backed by reserves, these systems expand or contract the token supply automatically based on market demand, often using a secondary token to absorb volatility. When the price rises above the peg, the protocol mints more tokens to push it down; when it falls below the peg, the protocol incentivizes reducing supply.

Example

A protocol might let users burn its stablecoin to mint a discounted amount of a secondary token whenever the peg drops, encouraging traders to buy back and help restore the price.

Why It Matters

Algorithmic designs aim to avoid dependence on centralized reserves, but they introduce mechanism-based risks that differ from collateral-backed stablecoins.

Risks / Limitations

Several algorithmic stablecoins have lost their peg during periods of heavy selling, sometimes collapsing entirely when the balancing mechanism could not keep up with demand.

All-Time High (ATH)

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

The all-time high is the highest price an asset has ever reached since it started trading.

How It Works

Exchanges and data aggregators track historical price data continuously, and the ATH updates automatically whenever a new peak is recorded.

Example

If Bitcoin trades above every previous recorded price on a given day, that new price becomes its ATH.

Why It Matters

Traders often reference the ATH as a psychological benchmark, since prices approaching a previous peak can affect market sentiment and trading activity.

Related Terms

  • All-Time Low (ATL)
  • Bull Market

All-Time Low (ATL)

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

The all-time low is the lowest price an asset has recorded since it began trading.

How It Works

Like the ATH, this figure is tracked from historical price data and updates whenever the price falls below every prior recorded low.

Example

A token that drops below its previous lowest recorded price on an exchange sets a new ATL.

Why It Matters

The ATL can offer context for how much an asset has recovered or declined over its lifetime, though it says nothing about future performance.

Related Terms

  • All-Time High (ATH)

Altcoin

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

An altcoin is any cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin.

How It Works

The term groups together thousands of different projects — from large networks like Ethereum to small, experimental tokens — that share only the trait of not being Bitcoin. Some altcoins aim to improve on Bitcoin's design, while others serve entirely different purposes, such as smart contracts or governance.

Example

Ethereum, Solana, and Litecoin are all considered altcoins.

Why It Matters

Because altcoins vary enormously in purpose, technology, and risk, treating them as a single category can be misleading — each project needs its own research.

Annual Percentage Yield (APY)

DeFi & Trading Intermediate

Simple Definition

APY is a rate that expresses how much a deposit or investment earns in a year, including the effect of compounding.

How It Works

When rewards are reinvested and start earning their own returns, total annual growth is higher than a simple rate would suggest. APY accounts for this compounding, typically assuming rewards are reinvested at the same rate throughout the year.

Example

A product offering 1% weekly rewards, if compounded, could show an APY well above the simple annual total of 52%.

Why It Matters

Comparing APY figures helps users evaluate yield-generating products like staking or liquidity pools on a like-for-like basis.

Risks / Limitations

High APY figures in crypto often come from inflationary token rewards rather than sustainable returns, and rates can change without notice.

Related Terms

  • Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

DeFi & Trading Beginner

Simple Definition

APR is a rate that shows the simple annual return on a deposit or investment, without accounting for compounding.

How It Works

APR is calculated by taking a periodic rate — say, a monthly reward — and multiplying it by the number of periods in a year, without assuming the rewards are reinvested.

Example

A lending platform offering 0.5% per month would quote a 6% APR, even if actual returns end up higher once compounding is factored in.

Why It Matters

Because APR excludes compounding, it is usually lower than the equivalent APY, and understanding the difference prevents mismatched comparisons between products.

Related Terms

  • Annual Percentage Yield (APY)

API

Technology & Infrastructure Beginner

Simple Definition

An API, or application programming interface, is a set of rules that lets different software programs communicate with each other.

How It Works

In crypto, APIs let developers pull data such as prices, order books, or wallet balances from an exchange or blockchain, or send instructions like placing a trade, without using the graphical interface directly.

Example

A trading bot might use an exchange's API to check an asset's current price and automatically place an order when certain conditions are met.

Why It Matters

APIs power much of the crypto ecosystem behind the scenes, from portfolio trackers to automated trading tools and block explorers.

Arbitrage

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

Arbitrage is the practice of buying an asset on one market and simultaneously selling it on another where the price is higher, profiting from the difference.

How It Works

Because crypto trades across many exchanges with independent order books, prices for the same asset can briefly diverge. Traders — often using automated systems — buy where the price is lower and sell where it is higher, and this activity tends to push prices back toward alignment.

Example

If a token trades at $10.00 on one exchange and $10.05 on another, an arbitrage trader could buy on the first and sell on the second to capture the five-cent difference, before fees.

Why It Matters

Arbitrage helps keep prices consistent across markets and is a core activity for many professional and automated trading strategies.

Risks / Limitations

Transaction fees, withdrawal delays, and slippage can erase the profit margin, and price gaps often close before a trade can be completed.

ASIC

Mining & Hardware Intermediate

Simple Definition

An ASIC, or application-specific integrated circuit, is a computer chip built to perform one task extremely efficiently — in crypto, that task is usually mining.

How It Works

Unlike general-purpose processors, ASICs are designed and manufactured solely to run a specific mining algorithm, such as Bitcoin's SHA-256. This specialization makes them far faster and more energy-efficient at that one job than regular computer hardware.

Example

Bitcoin mining today is dominated by dedicated ASIC machines rather than ordinary computers, since ASICs can perform the required calculations at a much higher rate.

Why It Matters

ASICs shape the mining landscape by raising the resources needed to compete, which influences how decentralized a network's mining ecosystem stays.

Risks / Limitations

ASIC hardware is expensive, consumes significant electricity, and becomes outdated quickly as newer, more efficient models are released.

Ask Price

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

The ask price is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept for an asset.

How It Works

Exchanges display an order book listing all outstanding buy and sell orders. The ask price sits at the top of the sell-side orders, and a trade executes when a buyer accepts that price or a seller accepts a buyer's bid.

Example

If the lowest sell order for a token is $2.50, that is the current ask price — a market buy order would fill at or near that level.

Why It Matters

The gap between the ask price and the highest buy offer, known as the spread, reflects market liquidity and trading costs.

Atomic Swap

Blockchain Fundamentals Advanced

Simple Definition

An atomic swap is a method for exchanging two different cryptocurrencies directly between two parties, without using a centralized exchange.

How It Works

The swap uses a smart-contract technique that ensures both sides of the trade happen together or not at all — if one party fails to complete their half, the transaction is automatically reversed. This is typically achieved through time-locked contracts that only release funds when both parties provide a matching cryptographic proof.

Example

Two users could trade Bitcoin for Litecoin directly between their own wallets using an atomic swap protocol, with neither side needing to trust the other or a middleman.

Why It Matters

Atomic swaps demonstrate how cross-chain trading can happen without custodial exchanges, reducing counterparty risk.

Risks / Limitations

Atomic swaps require both blockchains to support compatible scripting features, and the process can be slower and more technically complex than trading on an exchange.

Related Terms

  • AMM

Automated Market Maker

DeFi & Trading Intermediate

Simple Definition

An automated market maker, or AMM, is a system used by decentralized exchanges to set prices and execute trades using a mathematical formula instead of a traditional order book.

How It Works

Users called liquidity providers deposit pairs of tokens into a shared pool. A formula — commonly one that keeps the product of the two token quantities constant — determines the exchange rate as traders buy and sell against the pool. As one token is bought, its price rises relative to the other, automatically balancing supply and demand without a human market maker or matching buyers and sellers directly.

Example

A trader swapping ETH for a stablecoin on a decentralized exchange is trading against an AMM pool rather than against another person's order.

Why It Matters

AMMs allow anyone to trade or provide liquidity permissionlessly, removing the need for a centralized order book and enabling markets for thousands of token pairs, including many with low trading volume.

Risks / Limitations

Liquidity providers can experience impermanent loss when token prices diverge, and pools with low liquidity can produce significant price slippage on larger trades.

Related Terms

  • AMM
  • Bonding Curve
  • Arbitrage

Avalanche Effect

Cryptography Advanced

Simple Definition

The avalanche effect describes how a tiny change to input data — even a single character — produces a completely different, unpredictable output when run through a cryptographic hash function.

How It Works

Well-designed hash functions are built so that altering one bit of input cascades through the calculation, changing roughly half of the output bits. This makes it impossible to predict how a hash will change based on a small edit to the original data.

Example

Hashing the word "block" and the word "blocm" with SHA-256 produces two completely unrelated strings of characters, even though only one letter changed.

Why It Matters

The avalanche effect is a key property that makes blockchain hashing secure, since it prevents attackers from making small, predictable adjustments to forge transactions or blocks.

Bear Market

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

A bear market describes an extended period of falling prices across an asset or the broader market, generally accompanied by pessimistic sentiment.

How It Works

Bear markets often emerge from a combination of factors such as reduced demand, negative news, or macroeconomic pressure, and can last anywhere from weeks to years. It reflects a sustained shift in overall market direction rather than a short-term dip.

Example

A market where the prices of major cryptocurrencies fall significantly and stay depressed for many months would typically be described as a bear market.

Why It Matters

Recognizing bear market conditions helps investors think about risk management and time horizon rather than reacting to short-term price swings.

Related Terms

  • Bull Market
  • Bear Trap
  • All-Time Low (ATL)

Bear Trap

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

A bear trap is a price pattern where an asset appears to be breaking down into a decline, tempting traders to sell, before reversing sharply upward.

How It Works

The pattern typically forms when price drops below a recognized support level, triggering stop-loss orders and short-selling, but then quickly recovers — leaving sellers at a loss and the price higher than before the move.

Example

A token might briefly dip below a well-watched price level, prompting a wave of selling, only to rebound within hours as buyers step back in.

Why It Matters

Recognizing bear traps can help traders avoid reacting to short-lived price moves that don't reflect a genuine trend change.

Risks / Limitations

Bear traps are only identifiable in hindsight with certainty; no indicator can reliably predict one in advance.

Bitcoin

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

Bitcoin is the first and largest cryptocurrency, a decentralized digital currency that allows people to send value to each other without relying on a bank or central authority.

How It Works

Bitcoin runs on a public blockchain — a shared, continuously updated ledger maintained by a global network of independent computers. New transactions are grouped into blocks and added to this ledger through mining, where participants called miners compete using computing power to solve a mathematical puzzle under a system known as Proof of Work. The winning miner adds the next block and receives newly created bitcoin plus transaction fees as a reward, and the network reaches agreement on the ledger's state without any single party being in charge.

Example

Sending bitcoin to another person means broadcasting a transaction to the network, which miners then verify and include in a block, after which it becomes part of the permanent, public transaction history.

Why It Matters

Bitcoin introduced the first working model of decentralized digital money with a fixed maximum supply, and it remains the largest cryptocurrency by market value, often used as a reference point for the rest of the market.

Risks / Limitations

Bitcoin's price has historically been volatile, transaction confirmation can take time during network congestion, and mining consumes significant electricity.

Bitcoin Dominance

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

Bitcoin dominance is a metric showing what percentage of the total cryptocurrency market's value is made up of Bitcoin.

How It Works

It is calculated by dividing Bitcoin's market capitalization by the combined market capitalization of all cryptocurrencies. The figure moves as Bitcoin's price and supply change relative to the rest of the market.

Example

If Bitcoin dominance sits at 50%, that means Bitcoin accounts for roughly half of the total value held across all tracked cryptocurrencies.

Why It Matters

Traders sometimes use shifts in dominance as one of several inputs when thinking about whether capital appears to be moving toward Bitcoin or toward altcoins.

Risks / Limitations

The metric depends on which coins a data provider includes, can be skewed by a handful of very large assets, and does not by itself explain why capital is moving.

Related Terms

Bitcoin Halving

Tokenomics Intermediate

Simple Definition

The Bitcoin halving is a programmed event that cuts the reward miners receive for adding a new block in half, occurring roughly every four years.

How It Works

Bitcoin's protocol is designed to reduce the block reward by 50% after every 210,000 blocks are mined, which slows the rate at which new bitcoin enters circulation. This continues on a fixed schedule until the maximum supply of 21 million bitcoin is approached, at which point new issuance effectively stops.

Example

The block reward has moved from 50 bitcoin per block at launch down through several halvings to a fraction of that amount today, with the schedule continuing on a predictable, fixed cadence.

Why It Matters

Because halvings directly reduce new supply issuance, market participants watch them closely as a structural change to Bitcoin's supply schedule. The halving does not automatically cause any specific price outcome.

Risks / Limitations

A halving also reduces miner revenue from block rewards, which can affect mining profitability and, over time, network hash rate.

Related Terms

  • Block Reward
  • Bitcoin
  • Circulating Supply

Bitcoin ETF

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

A Bitcoin ETF is an exchange-traded fund that tracks the price of Bitcoin, allowing investors to gain exposure through a traditional brokerage account instead of buying and storing bitcoin directly.

How It Works

The fund issuer holds bitcoin, or bitcoin-linked instruments, on investors' behalf and issues shares that trade on a stock exchange, with the share price designed to track Bitcoin's market price. Investors buy and sell these shares like any other listed security.

Example

An investor could buy shares of a Bitcoin ETF through a standard brokerage account rather than setting up a crypto wallet and exchange account.

Why It Matters

Bitcoin ETFs give investors a regulated, familiar way to gain price exposure to Bitcoin without managing private keys or custody themselves.

Risks / Limitations

ETF shares typically carry management fees, may not track the underlying price perfectly, and investors do not hold the underlying bitcoin directly.

Related Terms

Block

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

A block is a batch of verified transactions bundled together and added as a single entry to a blockchain.

How It Works

Each block contains a set of transactions, a timestamp, and a reference to the previous block, linking them together in a chain. Once added, a block's contents become extremely difficult to alter without changing every subsequent block.

Example

A Bitcoin block might contain several thousand individual transactions, all confirmed together at the same time.

Why It Matters

Blocks are the fundamental unit that gives blockchain technology its name and its tamper-resistant structure.

Block Explorer

Technology & Infrastructure Beginner

Simple Definition

A block explorer is a website or tool that lets anyone search and view the public data recorded on a blockchain, such as transactions, addresses, and blocks.

How It Works

The explorer reads data directly from the blockchain and presents it in a searchable, human-readable format, showing details like transaction status, wallet balances, and confirmation counts.

Example

Entering a transaction ID into a block explorer shows whether the transaction has confirmed, how many confirmations it has, and which addresses were involved.

Why It Matters

Block explorers give users an independent way to verify transactions without relying on a wallet provider or exchange's reporting.

Block Height

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

Block height is the number of blocks that have been added to a blockchain before a given block, counting from the very first block.

How It Works

The first block in a chain, known as the genesis block, has a height of zero, and each subsequent block increases the count by one.

Example

A blockchain at block height 800,000 has had 800,000 blocks added since its genesis block.

Why It Matters

Block height gives a precise, unambiguous way to reference a specific point in a blockchain's history, which is useful for developers and for tracking network progress.

Block Reward

Mining & Hardware Beginner

Simple Definition

The block reward is the payment a miner or validator receives for successfully adding a new block to the blockchain.

How It Works

The reward usually combines newly created coins with the transaction fees collected from that block, compensating participants for the computing power or capital they contribute to securing the network.

Example

Bitcoin miners currently receive a fixed amount of newly minted bitcoin plus the fees from all the transactions included in the block they add.

Why It Matters

Block rewards are the economic incentive that keeps miners or validators participating, which underpins the network's security.

Blockchain

Blockchain Fundamentals Beginner

Simple Definition

A blockchain is a distributed digital ledger that records transactions across a network of computers in a way that makes past entries very difficult to alter.

How It Works

Instead of one central party keeping the records, many independent computers, called nodes, each hold a copy of the ledger. New transactions are grouped into blocks, and the network uses a consensus mechanism to agree on which block gets added next. Each block references the previous one, forming a chronological chain — if someone tried to alter an old block, the change would break that link and stand out immediately to the rest of the network.

Example

Picture a shared spreadsheet copied across thousands of computers worldwide. When someone adds a new row of transactions, every copy updates to match once the network agrees the new row is valid, and no single computer can secretly edit an old row without the rest rejecting it.

Why It Matters

This structure allows blockchains to maintain a trustworthy, shared record of transactions without requiring a bank, government, or company to manage it centrally.

Blockchain Trilemma

Blockchain Fundamentals Advanced

Simple Definition

The blockchain trilemma is the idea that blockchain networks struggle to fully maximize decentralization, security, and scalability at the same time, often improving one at the expense of another.

How It Works

A highly decentralized, secure network tends to process transactions more slowly because many independent participants must validate and agree on each change. Increasing transaction speed and capacity often requires reducing the number of validating participants or simplifying validation, which can weaken decentralization or security.

Example

A blockchain that limits its validator set to increase transaction speed may process transactions faster than one with thousands of independent validators, but at the cost of relying on fewer parties.

Why It Matters

Understanding this trade-off helps explain why different blockchains make different design choices, and why no single network claims to be optimal on all three dimensions simultaneously.

  • Bridge

Blue Chip Crypto

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

Blue chip crypto refers informally to established, large-cap cryptocurrencies with a long track record and significant market presence, most commonly Bitcoin and Ethereum.

How It Works

The term borrows from traditional stock market language, where "blue chip" describes large, well-known companies. In crypto, it's generally applied to assets with substantial history, liquidity, and market capitalization compared to newer or smaller projects.

Example

Investors researching the space might compare a small, recently launched token against blue chip assets like Bitcoin to understand relative risk.

Why It Matters

The label is used as informal shorthand for relative market maturity, not a guarantee of stability or future performance.

Risks / Limitations

There is no official definition or list of blue chip cryptocurrencies, and even large-cap assets remain considerably more volatile than traditional blue chip stocks.

Related Terms

Bollinger Bands

Technical Analysis Intermediate

Simple Definition

Bollinger Bands are a technical analysis tool made up of three lines plotted on a price chart — a moving average in the middle, with an upper and lower band set a certain distance above and below it based on price volatility.

How It Works

The bands widen when an asset's price is more volatile and narrow when price movement is calmer. Some traders watch for price touching or moving outside the bands as a sign of potentially overextended conditions, though this is one input among many, not a standalone prediction tool.

Example

A trader might note that a token's price touching the upper band during a rally suggests short-term price action is stretched relative to its recent average, without assuming a reversal is guaranteed.

Why It Matters

Bollinger Bands give traders a visual way to gauge relative volatility and price extremes over time.

Risks / Limitations

Like all technical indicators, Bollinger Bands are based on historical price data and are not a guaranteed predictor of future price direction.

Related Terms

  • Bull Trap

Bonding Curve

Tokenomics Advanced

Simple Definition

A bonding curve is a mathematical formula that automatically determines a token's price based on its current supply, used to issue or redeem tokens without a traditional order book.

How It Works

As more tokens are purchased through the curve, the formula raises the price for the next buyer; as tokens are sold back, the price decreases. This creates continuous, algorithmic liquidity, since the smart contract itself acts as the counterparty for every trade.

Example

A project might use a bonding curve where each new token minted costs slightly more than the last, rewarding early participants while giving later buyers a transparent, predictable pricing formula.

Why It Matters

Bonding curves allow new tokens to have built-in liquidity and price discovery from launch, without needing external market makers.

Risks / Limitations

Poorly designed curves can be exploited or can create extreme price swings on relatively small trades.

Related Terms

  • Automated Market Maker
  • Annual Percentage Yield (APY)

Bridge

Blockchain Fundamentals Intermediate

Simple Definition

A bridge is a protocol that allows assets or data to move between two otherwise separate blockchains.

How It Works

Because most blockchains operate independently and cannot natively read each other's data, bridges typically work by locking or burning an asset on the original chain while minting a corresponding representation on the destination chain, or by coordinating a swap between the two.

Example

A user wanting to use an Ethereum-based token on another blockchain might send it through a bridge, receiving a wrapped version of the token that can be used on the destination network.

Why It Matters

Bridges make it possible for liquidity and applications to move across the increasingly fragmented landscape of separate blockchain networks.

Risks / Limitations

Bridges have historically been a common target for exploits, since they often concentrate large amounts of locked value in a single smart contract or trusted intermediary.

Related Terms

  • Atomic Swap
  • Blockchain Trilemma

Bull Market

Trading & Market Data Beginner

Simple Definition

A bull market describes an extended period of rising prices, typically accompanied by growing investor confidence.

How It Works

Bull markets can be driven by factors such as increased adoption, positive news, or broader economic conditions, and like bear markets, they reflect a sustained trend rather than a single day's price move.

Example

A period where major cryptocurrencies see sustained price increases over many months would typically be described as a bull market.

Why It Matters

Recognizing overall market trend helps investors contextualize price action, though it offers no guarantee about what happens next.

Related Terms

  • Bear Market
  • Bull Trap
  • All-Time High (ATH)

Bull Trap

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

A bull trap is a price pattern where an asset appears to be breaking out into a rally, encouraging traders to buy, before reversing sharply downward.

How It Works

The pattern often forms when price pushes above a recognized resistance level, triggering buy orders, but then fails to sustain the move and falls back below that level — leaving buyers at a loss.

Example

A token might briefly rise above a closely watched price level, prompting a wave of buying, only to fall back below it within a short period.

Why It Matters

Being aware of bull traps encourages traders to look for confirmation before acting on a single price breakout.

Risks / Limitations

As with bear traps, a bull trap can typically only be confirmed after the fact.

Related Terms

  • Bear Trap
  • Bull Market

Burn / Token Burn

Tokenomics Beginner

Simple Definition

A token burn is the permanent removal of a cryptocurrency from circulation, reducing the total available supply.

How It Works

Tokens are usually burned by sending them to a wallet address that cannot be accessed by anyone, effectively taking them out of circulation forever. Some projects burn tokens on a set schedule, while others tie burns to network activity, such as a portion of transaction fees.

Example

A network might automatically burn a small amount of its native token with every transaction processed, gradually reducing total supply over time.

Why It Matters

Token burns are one tool projects use to manage supply, and are sometimes framed as a way to offset new token issuance.

Risks / Limitations

A burn does not guarantee any particular price effect, since price also depends on demand and many other market factors.

Related Terms

  • Circulating Supply
  • Bitcoin Halving

Buy Wall

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

A buy wall is a large cluster of buy orders placed at a specific price on an exchange's order book, creating a visible barrier that can slow a price decline.

How It Works

When a large number of buy orders sit at one price level, an asset falling toward that price needs enough selling pressure to absorb all those orders before it can fall further, which can temporarily support the price.

Example

A trader watching an order book might notice a very large buy order sitting just below the current price, forming a visible wall on the chart.

Why It Matters

Buy walls give traders a sense of where significant demand is concentrated, though they can be removed or altered instantly by the party who placed them.

Risks / Limitations

Buy walls can be used to create a false impression of demand and are sometimes cancelled before ever being filled, a practice known as spoofing.

Related Terms

  • Ask Price
  • Arbitrage

Candlestick

A trader might notice a large cluster of historical volume around a particular price level on a volume profile chart and treat that zone as an area worth watching for future support or resistance.

Virtual Machine

Technology & Infrastructure Intermediate

Simple Definition

A virtual machine, in the blockchain context, is the software environment that executes smart contract code and processes the resulting changes to the network's state.

How It Works

Rather than running smart contract code directly on each computer's native operating system, a blockchain's virtual machine provides a consistent, isolated environment that runs the same way on every node in the network, ensuring all participants reach the same result when executing the same code. Example

The Ethereum Virtual Machine, or EVM, is the most widely used blockchain virtual machine, and many other networks have built EVM-compatible environments so that applications developed for Ethereum can run on them with little modification.

Wallet

Wallets & Security Beginner

Simple Definition

A crypto wallet is software or hardware that stores the private keys needed to access and control cryptocurrency recorded on a blockchain, rather than physically holding the coins the way a leather wallet holds cash.

How It Works

Because crypto assets exist as entries on a distributed ledger rather than as physical objects, a wallet's real job is managing the private keys that prove ownership. A wallet generates a public address for receiving funds and uses the matching private key to produce the digital signature required to authorize an outgoing transaction. Wallets are generally described as custodial, where a third party such as an exchange holds the keys on a user's behalf, or non-custodial, where the user alone holds and is responsible for the keys.

Example

A person might use a mobile app wallet for everyday spending and a separate hardware wallet to store larger holdings for the long term.

Why It Matters

Understanding how wallets work is fundamental to using crypto safely, since access to funds depends entirely on control of the private key rather than on any physical possession.

Risks / Limitations

Losing a private key or seed phrase without a backup typically means the associated funds cannot be recovered. Custodial wallets introduce counterparty risk, since funds depend on the security and solvency of the provider holding the keys.

Wash Trading

Trading & Market Data Intermediate

Simple Definition

Wash trading is the practice of simultaneously buying and selling the same asset, often through connected or coordinated accounts, to create the appearance of trading activity without any genuine change in ownership.

How It Works

Rather than reflecting real supply and demand between independent participants, wash trades are typically placed by the same party or by accounts acting in coordination, matching buy and sell orders against each other. This can inflate an asset's reported trading volume without transferring meaningful economic risk between parties.

Why It Matters

Trading volume is commonly used as a proxy for liquidity and genuine market interest. When volume is artificially inflated, it can distort how an asset or exchange is ranked and evaluated by traders and data platforms.

Risks / Limitations

Wash trading can mislead market participants about real liquidity, attract regulatory scrutiny, and undermine confidence in reported market data. It is treated as manipulative activity by most regulated exchanges and jurisdictions.

Decentralized exchanges, lending protocols, and NFT marketplaces are commonly described as examples of the Web3 application category.

Web3 represents an alternative model to app ecosystems built around centralized platforms that control user data and access, shifting more control toward individual wallet holders and open protocols.

The Web3 ecosystem is still developing, with ongoing challenges around user experience, scalability, and regulation. It is best understood as an evolving set of technologies and applications rather than a wholesale replacement of the existing internet.

A wrapped version of an asset can allow holders to use that asset's value within decentralized applications built on a network different from the asset's native blockchain.

Wrapping extends the practical utility of an asset by letting its value move into ecosystems it wasn't originally designed to interact with.

Zero-Knowledge Proof

Cryptography Intermediate

Simple Definition

A zero-knowledge proof is a cryptographic method that allows one party to prove a statement is true to another party without revealing the underlying information behind that statement.

How It Works

A simple conceptual example is proving that you know a password without ever revealing the password itself, by successfully responding to a series of challenges that only someone who actually knows it could pass. Zero-knowledge proof systems formalize this idea mathematically, allowing a verifier to become confident a statement holds without learning anything else about it.

Example

In a blockchain context, a zero-knowledge proof can be used to confirm that a batch of transactions was processed correctly without the verifier needing to re-execute every transaction individually.

Why It Matters

Zero-knowledge proofs enable privacy-preserving verification and more efficient confirmation of computation, both of which have become important building blocks in blockchain scaling and privacy technology.

Related Terms

  • zk-SNARK
  • zk-STARK
  • Zero-Knowledge Technology

zk-Rollup

Technology & Infrastructure Advanced

Simple Definition

A zk-rollup is a Layer 2 scaling method that bundles many transactions together off the main blockchain and submits a cryptographic proof to the base layer confirming they were processed correctly.

How It Works

Transactions are executed off-chain, and a validity proof, generated using zero-knowledge proof techniques, is posted to the base layer along with compressed transaction data. The base layer verifies this proof and settles the resulting state, without needing to re-execute each individual transaction itself.

Example

A zk-rollup can process a large batch of transfers off-chain and submit a single proof to Ethereum confirming that batch was carried out correctly.

Why It Matters

By moving computation off the base layer while still relying on it for security and final settlement, zk-rollups can meaningfully reduce the cost and increase the throughput of processing transactions.

Risks / Limitations

A zk-rollup differs from an optimistic rollup in an important way: optimistic rollups assume transactions are valid unless challenged within a dispute window, while zk-rollups submit a cryptographic proof of validity upfront, without relying on that challenge period.

Related Terms

  • Rollup
  • Optimistic Rollup
  • Layer 2
  • Zero-Knowledge Proof

zk-SNARK

Cryptography Advanced

Simple Definition

A zk-SNARK, short for Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-Interactive Argument of Knowledge, is a type of zero-knowledge proof system designed to produce proofs that are small in size and fast for a verifier to check.

How It Works

At a high level, a prover generates a compact proof that a statement or computation is correct, and a verifier can confirm that proof's validity without re-executing the underlying computation or needing an ongoing back-and-forth exchange with the prover.

Example

zk-SNARKs are used in some blockchain systems to verify that a set of transactions or a computation was carried out correctly, without exposing every underlying detail.

Why It Matters

The succinctness of zk-SNARK proofs makes it practical to verify complex computations cheaply, including on-chain, which is valuable for both scaling and privacy applications.

Related Terms

  • Zero-Knowledge Proof
  • zk-STARK
  • zk-Rollup

zk-STARK

Cryptography Advanced

Simple Definition

A zk-STARK, short for Zero-Knowledge Scalable Transparent Argument of Knowledge, is a zero-knowledge proof system designed with an emphasis on scalability and on avoiding reliance on a trusted setup process.

How It Works

"Transparent" refers to not requiring a trusted setup ceremony that some other proof systems depend on, instead relying on publicly verifiable randomness as part of generating and checking proofs.

Example

zk-STARKs can be used, similarly to other zero-knowledge proof systems, to verify that a computation or batch of transactions was carried out correctly.

Why It Matters

The transparency and scalability characteristics of zk-STARKs make them a distinct option within the broader zero-knowledge proof family, with different trade-offs than other approaches.

Risks / Limitations

Different zero-knowledge proof systems trade off differently across factors such as proof size, verification speed, and setup requirements. Specific performance claims should be verified against current, authoritative technical sources rather than assumed.

  • zk-Rollup

ZK-EVM

Technology & Infrastructure Advanced

Simple Definition

A ZK-EVM is a Layer 2 environment designed to be compatible with the Ethereum Virtual Machine while using zero-knowledge proofs to verify that transactions were executed correctly.

How It Works

A ZK-EVM lets smart contracts written for Ethereum run within a zk-rollup style system. As transactions are processed, the system generates validity proofs for batches of EVM-compatible activity and submits them back to Ethereum for verification.

Example

A developer can generally deploy an existing Ethereum smart contract onto a ZK-EVM environment with little to no modification, since it is built to behave in a compatible way.

Why It Matters

In beginner-friendly terms, a ZK-EVM helps zero-knowledge scaling technology work with the tools and smart contracts developers already use for Ethereum, rather than requiring an entirely separate, incompatible system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to common questions about crypto terminology.

A crypto glossary is a reference guide that explains the words, abbreviations, and concepts used across blockchain, cryptocurrency, and trading. It helps beginners and experienced users alike understand unfamiliar terms in plain language.

New users generally benefit most from learning blockchain, wallet, private key, exchange, market cap, and stablecoin first, since these concepts form the foundation for almost every other crypto term. The Beginner Quick Guide on this page walks through them in order.

A coin has its own independent blockchain, such as Bitcoin or Ether. A token is built on top of an existing blockchain, such as an ERC-20 token running on Ethereum, and relies on that network's infrastructure rather than maintaining its own.

A blockchain is a shared digital ledger that records transactions in linked blocks across a network of computers, rather than in one central database. Once a block is added, changing it requires agreement from the network, which makes the record difficult to alter.

DeFi, short for decentralized finance, refers to financial services such as lending, borrowing, and trading that run on blockchain smart contracts instead of traditional banks or brokers, letting users interact directly with the protocol.

A crypto wallet is software or hardware that stores the private keys needed to access and control cryptocurrency recorded on a blockchain. It does not hold the coins themselves the way a physical wallet holds cash.

Market capitalization is the total value of a cryptocurrency's circulating supply, calculated by multiplying the current price by the number of coins or tokens in circulation. It is commonly used to compare the relative size of different crypto assets.

ATH stands for all-time high, the highest price an asset has ever reached since it started trading. Traders often reference the distance between the current price and the ATH when discussing an asset's momentum.

HODL originated as a misspelling of "hold" in a 2013 Bitcoin forum post and became crypto slang for holding an asset through price swings rather than selling. It is now used broadly to describe a long-term, low-frequency-trading mindset.

Leverage lets a trader open a position larger than their own deposited funds by borrowing the difference from an exchange. It magnifies both potential gains and potential losses, and can lead to liquidation if the market moves against the position.

Liquidation is the forced closure of a leveraged trading position when losses reduce the trader's margin below the level required to keep it open. The exchange automatically closes the position to prevent losses from exceeding the funds put up as collateral.

Staking is the process of locking up cryptocurrency to help secure a proof-of-stake blockchain and validate transactions, in exchange for rewards paid by the network. It generally requires the asset to remain committed for a period of time.

A smart contract is self-executing code stored on a blockchain that automatically carries out predefined actions once its conditions are met, without needing a middleman to enforce the agreement.

A liquidity pool is a shared pot of two or more tokens locked in a smart contract that lets a decentralized exchange facilitate trades without a traditional order book. Users who deposit funds into the pool typically earn a share of the trading fees.

Tokenomics describes the economic design of a cryptocurrency, including its total supply, distribution, issuance schedule, and utility. It shapes how a token's supply and demand are expected to behave over time.

Web3 describes a vision for the internet built on decentralized blockchain networks, where users can hold direct ownership of digital assets and data through their own wallets rather than relying on centralized platforms.
